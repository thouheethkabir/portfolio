Ojas Café

A café in Palani, Tamil Nadu, with a 133-item menu its owner updates personally — no developer, no CMS, no monthly platform fee.

The brief

Ojas Café was opening in Palani and needed a site ready before launch night — somewhere Instagram traffic could land, read the full menu and get directions to the door.

One constraint shaped the whole build. The café is a small independent business, not a chain with a marketing team. Whoever runs it had to be able to change a price, add a dish or correct the opening hours themselves, on a laptop, without calling a developer and without paying a platform fee every month for the privilege.

The approach

I built it as hand-coded HTML, CSS and JavaScript rather than reaching for a site builder. A café menu is structured, repetitive data — exactly the thing a plain file handles well and a page builder makes tedious.

All 133 dishes live in a single text file. Changing a price means opening that file, editing a number and saving. A settings block at the top of the main script holds the phone number, WhatsApp link, Google Maps link, address, opening hours and the launch offer; fill those in once and every Call button, every Directions link, the footer and the Open now badge update across all four pages at once.

There is no build step and nothing to install. The whole site can be maintained in Notepad or TextEdit, and it deploys as static files — which is also why it costs nothing to host.

What I built

  • Four hand-coded pages — home, menu, gallery and visit
  • A 133-item menu driven by one owner-editable data file
  • A single settings block controlling contact details, hours and offers site-wide
  • Photo and video gallery
  • Visit page with map, address, hours and a live “Open now” badge
  • A holding page that ran until opening night
  • Mobile-first layouts, since most visitors arrive from Instagram
  • Written documentation so the owner can maintain it unaided

The outcome

The café owns its site outright. There is no subscription to lapse, no plugin to break and no vendor to depend on — and because it is static, it loads fast on the patchy mobile connections most of its customers are browsing on.

It is the same approach I used to build the site you are reading now.
